# Desk Glossary — Plain-English Reference

Added 2026-07-28. See OPERATING-INSTRUCTIONS.md §18. This is a living reference —
extend it when new recurring jargon appears in a brief; don't let it go stale.
Gloss a term inline (short parenthetical) on its first use in each report; this
file is the fuller explanation for anyone who wants it.

| Term | Plain-English meaning |
|---|---|
| Risk-off | Investors are becoming more cautious and shifting toward safer assets (cash, government bonds, gold) and away from riskier ones (stocks, high-yield credit, emerging markets). |
| Risk-on | The opposite — investors are more willing to take risk, buying stocks and riskier credit. |
| Stagflationary stress | Markets are worried about weak growth and stubborn inflation happening at the same time — the uncomfortable combination that limits what a central bank can do about either one without worsening the other. |
| Hawkish hold | A central bank left its policy rate unchanged, but signaled it's still leaning toward raising rates rather than cutting them. |
| Dovish hold | A central bank left its rate unchanged but signaled it's leaning toward easing (cutting) rather than tightening. |
| Bull flattening | Longer-term bond yields fell faster than shorter-term yields — usually reflects the market pricing in weaker long-run growth or inflation. |
| Bear steepening | Longer-term yields rose faster than short-term yields — often reflects worries about long-run inflation or the government issuing more debt (a "term premium" rising). |
| Cross-asset corroboration | Several different markets (stocks, bonds, currencies, commodities) are telling the same story, which makes the overall read more reliable. |
| Divergence / contradiction | Two markets or indicators that normally move together are instead pointing in different directions — worth investigating, since it flags that something unusual is happening. |
| Tail hedge | A position or asset (often gold, or out-of-the-money options) held as insurance against a severe, low-probability negative surprise. |
| Breadth | How many individual stocks are rising vs. falling. "Negative breadth" means the index went up even though more individual stocks fell than rose — the gain was concentrated in a few large names, not broad-based. |
| Priced in | The market's current prices already reflect an expectation — so even if the expected event happens exactly as predicted, prices may not move much, because everyone already positioned for it. |
| Invalidation (condition) | The specific future development that would prove a current interpretation, forecast, or scenario wrong. Naming it upfront is what makes a forecast falsifiable rather than just a vibe. |
| Confirmation signal | The specific future development that would support/strengthen a current interpretation or scenario, the mirror of an invalidation condition. |
| Credit impulse | The rate of change in new lending/credit growth (not the level) — whether credit is accelerating or decelerating, which tends to lead real-economy activity. |
| Terms of trade | Whether a country's exports are becoming more valuable relative to what it has to pay for its imports — a commodity exporter's terms of trade improve when that commodity's price rises; an importer's terms of trade worsen. |
| Real yield | A bond yield after subtracting expected inflation — the return an investor actually keeps in purchasing-power terms. |
| Carry trade | Borrowing in a currency with low interest rates to invest in a currency/asset with higher rates, pocketing the difference — profitable as long as the low-rate currency doesn't suddenly strengthen sharply. |
| Positioning | How heavily traders are already betting in one direction — matters because heavily "positioned" markets can move sharply if those bets have to be unwound. |
| Crowded trade | A bet that a very large share of traders are already making in the same direction — makes the market more vulnerable to a sharp reversal if the crowd tries to exit at once. |
| Open interest (OI) | The total number of outstanding futures/options contracts — rising OI alongside a price move suggests genuinely new money is entering, not just existing positions being repriced. |
| Volatility skew | Whether options that protect against a price fall are much more expensive than options that would profit from a price rise (or vice versa) — reveals which direction the market is more afraid of. |
| Basis point (bp) | One-hundredth of a percentage point (0.01%). Used because rate/spread moves are often too small to describe cleanly in whole percentages. |
| OAS (option-adjusted spread) | The extra yield a corporate bond pays over a comparable government bond, adjusted for any embedded options — a standard gauge of how much compensation investors demand for credit risk. |
| Sovereign CDS | A financial contract that pays out if a government defaults on its debt — its price is a market-based read on perceived default risk for that country. |
| SOFR / EFFR | Two US overnight interest-rate benchmarks (Secured Overnight Financing Rate; Effective Federal Funds Rate) — used to check whether short-term bank/money-market funding is behaving normally. |
| TACO pattern | Market shorthand ("Trump Always Chickens Out") for an observed cycle: a policy threat is announced, markets react, and the measure is later softened, delayed, or reversed. This desk tracks it as a documented behavioral pattern, not as a certainty that repeats every time — see the Political Reaction-Function Board. |
| Reaction-function board | A structured, evidence-based read on how much pain (market, political, legal) a policymaker can tolerate before changing course — described in qualitative bands (low/medium/high, rising/falling), never a made-up numeric score. |
| Regime | This desk's single overall label for the macro backdrop (e.g., risk-off, risk-on, stagflationary-stress) — changed only when evidence corroborates across at least two asset classes, not on one day's price action. |
| Scenario band | A probability range (never a false-precision single number) attached to a base/upside/downside macro scenario — reflects genuine uncertainty about which path plays out. |
| Tactical vs. strategic (sector rotation) | Tactical = short-term (days/weeks) relative performance; Strategic = longer-term (months) fit with the macro cycle. A sector can be tactically improving (rallying this week) while still strategically weak (poor multi-month fundamentals), or vice versa. |
